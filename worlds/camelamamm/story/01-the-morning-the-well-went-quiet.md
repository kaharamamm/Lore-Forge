---
title: The Morning the Well Went Quiet
order: 1
date: 10.03.512
---

Every morning in Camelamamm began with a song, and every morning Mr Camel was awake to hear it.

He liked to be on the roof of the Patient Kettle before the sun. He would set the brass kettle on its little stove, fold his long legs under him on the warm tiles, and wait. Below him the city lay blue and quiet: the sand-coloured houses with their blue doors, the stepped terraces of palms, the copper dome of the palace on its hill still holding a little of the night. Then, as the first light touched the dome, a low note would rise out of the middle of the city, so deep that he felt it in his hump before he heard it with his ears. It was the Singing Well, drawing up its water from far under the desert, and the whole city woke to it the way a child wakes to its mother humming.

The kettle always began to sing a moment later, as if it were answering. Mr Camel had never decided whether that was the kettle's idea or his own.

On the tenth morning of Highheat he folded his legs, set out his glass, and waited, and the well did not sing.

At first he thought he had missed it. He was not as young as he had been, and there was a grey tuft between his ears now that had not been there when he opened the tea house. He tilted his head and listened harder. He heard a dog. He heard a shutter bang somewhere on the hill. He heard, very faintly, the palms rubbing their leaves together in the dawn wind. He did not hear the well.

The sun came over the edge of the Sunsea, gold and enormous, and lit the whole city at once. The kettle began to whistle on its stove. Mr Camel took it off the flame and did not pour.

"Well," he said to nobody. "That is new."

By the time he came downstairs, the Street of Kettles was full of people standing in their doorways in their nightclothes, all looking the same way, towards the round stone canopy at the heart of the city. Nobody was shouting. That was the strangest part. Camelamamm was a city that shouted about everything, about the price of dates and the colour of a neighbour's door and whose turn it was to sweep the steps, and this morning it was perfectly silent, as if the whole city were holding its breath to listen for a sound that would not come.

Mr Camel walked to the well with his hands folded behind his back. The crowd let him through. People in Camelamamm let him through most places; he was tall, and slow, and they had learned that it was easier to step aside than to wait for him to finish stepping.

The Singing Well was six strides across, a ring of green-glazed bricks under a stone canopy carved with water-birds. On an ordinary morning its water stood a hand below the rim, clear and silver and very slightly trembling, like a held note. This morning Mr Camel leaned over the edge and saw the water far down in the dark, flat and dull as a coin. A woman beside him lowered a bucket on its rope, and the rope went down and down before it splashed.

"It has dropped the height of a camel since midnight," said the old bucket-seller who had his stall by the steps. "I measured. I always measure."

"Then you are a wise man," said Mr Camel, "and I am glad of you."

He stood there for a long time. People began to talk again in low voices, and then in higher ones. Somebody said the well was angry. Somebody else said it was a curse from the Glass Dunes, where lightning had struck for a hundred years and left the sand fused into green glass. A boy said the well had simply got tired of singing, which Mr Camel privately thought was the most sensible suggestion he had heard.

He was turning to go home when something small and fast hit him in the knees.

"There you are," said Pip, picking herself up. "I have been to the Kettle twice. You were not on the roof. You are always on the roof."

Pip was a fennec, a palace courier, and the only person in Camelamamm who had never once stepped aside for Mr Camel. She was small and sandy and mostly ears, and she wore a blue courier sash with the palace seal on it and a satchel so big that it bumped against the backs of her knees when she ran, which was always. There was a notch in her right ear from a wall she should not have climbed. She came to the Patient Kettle every afternoon to tell Mr Camel he was too slow, and he poured her tea every afternoon and agreed with her.

"I came to listen to the well," said Mr Camel. "It is not singing."

"I know it is not singing. Everybody knows it is not singing. That is why I am here." She pulled a letter out of the satchel, sealed in saffron wax with the copper crown pressed into it. "The Queen wants you. Now, she said. And then she said, no, tell him to finish his tea first or he will be no use to anybody. And then she said, but tell him to drink it fast."

Mr Camel took the letter and turned it over in his long fingers without opening it. He did not need to. He had known Samira since they were both young and loading caravans at the edge of the Sunsea, before anybody thought of putting a circlet on her head. If she was writing to him on the morning the well went quiet, there was only one thing she could want.

"I have not had my tea," he said.

Pip looked at him with her enormous amber eyes. "Mr Camel. The well."

"Yes," he said gently. "The well. Which is why I will need my tea." And he went home, and climbed the stairs, and poured the first glass from the kettle, which had gone a little cold. He drank it slowly, all the way to the bottom, while Pip hopped from foot to foot in the doorway, and then he put on his green fez and went to see the Queen.
