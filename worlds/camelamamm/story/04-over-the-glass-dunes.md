---
title: Over the Glass Dunes
order: 4
date: 11.03.512
---

Tobiah's skiff was called the Grumbling Gull, and it had earned the name.

It was a long palm-wood sled on brass runners with a patched red sail and a gull painted on the bow, one eye larger than the other, so that it always seemed to be looking at you sideways. It creaked when it stood still. It creaked louder when it moved. It leaked sand from places sand had no business being. Tobiah, who was short and barrel-chested and wore his goggles on his forehead and his grey beard in every direction at once, had flown it across the Sunsea for seventeen years and had never once, he told them, been asked to take it over the Glass Dunes in the middle of the night by a tea merchant and a fox.

"Fennec," said Pip.

"In the middle of the night," said Tobiah, as if she had not spoken, "with no water, no supplies, and a map that hums." He hauled on a rope and the red sail filled with the cold desert wind and the Gull lurched forward with a groan. "That is eleven years of tea, Mr Camel. I want it written down that this pays for all of it."

"It pays for all of it," Mr Camel agreed, folding himself into the back of the skiff with the map tube across his knees. "I will even give you the third glass free."

They left Camelamamm before dawn on the eleventh, sliding out of the south gate past the dry channels where the water should have been, and by sunrise the city was a blue smudge behind them and there was nothing in front but the gold of the Sunsea, rolling away under a sky so wide it made Pip's ears ache. The skiff flew. It skimmed down the long backs of the dunes so fast that the runners sang, and climbed the next slope slow and creaking, and tipped over the top and flew again. Pip sat on the bow beside the painted gull with the wind flattening her ears and laughed out loud for the first time since the well went quiet.

Every hour Mr Camel unrolled the map a little and put his ear to it. The humming was always there, far to the south, bright and steady in the little blue hollow under the Glass. Everywhere else the silver lines were silent.

By the afternoon of the second day the sand under the runners began to change. It grew darker, and harder, and it began to glitter. Then the first ridge of glass rose out of the dunes ahead of them, a long green wave frozen in the act of breaking, taller than the palace dome, and the wind blowing across its edge made a sound like a thousand people running wet fingers round a thousand glasses at once.

"The Glass Dunes," said Tobiah, and for once he did not complain about anything.

Long ago, Ozra had told Mr Camel, lightning had struck here every night for a hundred years, and where it struck the sand had melted and cooled into glass. Nobody knew why. The caravans went around. The sand-skiffs went over, if the pilot was brave or owed somebody a favour, and they went fast, because the glass ridges were sharp and the hollows between them were deep, and the wind here did not behave like wind anywhere else.

They were halfway across when the storm came.

It came out of the south like a wall, and it was not a wall of sand but of glitter: billions of tiny flakes of glass whipped up off the ridges and thrown through the air, catching the low sun so that the whole sky turned to blinding, spinning light. It roared and it rang at the same time. Tobiah yanked his goggles down over his eyes and screamed at the sail. Mr Camel wrapped his scarf around his face and pulled Pip down into the bottom of the skiff and wrapped his long arms around her, map tube and all, and closed his eyes, because there was no point in keeping them open.

"I cannot see!" Tobiah bellowed. "I cannot see a thing! If we hit a ridge we will be in a thousand pieces!"

Mr Camel did not answer. He was listening.

Under the roar of the storm, under the ringing of the glass, so faint that he would never have heard it if he had not spent fourteen years listening for the well at dawn, there was a hum. He unrolled the map against his chest, inside his coat, and put his ear to it, and it was there: the water, bright and steady, a little to the left of where they were going.

"Left," he said.

"What?"

"A little to the left, Tobiah. And down. There is a hollow there, the map is humming, there is water under it. Glass does not form over running water."

Tobiah swore, and hauled on the tiller, and the Gull swung left with a scream of runners and plunged down into a hollow that none of them could see. For one long moment they were falling. Then the runners hit something smooth and the whole skiff spun round twice and slid to a stop, and the storm went on roaring over their heads, high above them, while they sat in a deep, still bowl of sand at the bottom of the dunes with the light flickering green above them like the surface of a pool seen from underneath.

Pip lifted her head out of Mr Camel's coat. Her ears were full of glitter.

"You were slow," she said shakily. "You were really, really slow."

"I was listening," said Mr Camel.

"I know." She wiped her nose on her sash. "It was good slow."

When the storm blew itself out, the stars came out over the Glass Dunes, and the glass ridges all around them caught the starlight and held it, so that they sat in a ring of soft green fire. And in the side of the hollow, half-hidden behind a lip of melted sand, there was a dark opening in the glass, and from somewhere deep inside it, very faint, they could all hear the sound of dripping water, coming back to them seven times.
