---
title: The Steward of Water
order: 2
date: "10.03.512"
---

The palace council sat in a long cool room under the copper dome, and when Mr Camel ducked through its door every one of them stopped talking.

Queen Samira was not on her throne. She was sitting on the steps below it with her saffron robe tucked around her riding trousers and her long braid over one shoulder, the copper rings in it clicking when she turned her head. She looked tired. She also looked, for a moment, very glad.

"You finished your tea," she said.

"The first glass," said Mr Camel. "The second and third are waiting for me. They are patient."

"Then I hope you are too, because I need you to listen to my Steward of Water." She pointed her riding crop down the long table. "Malquor. Tell him what you told us."

Malquor stood. He was a tall, narrow man in a grey silk coat buttoned all the way to his throat, with silver hair combed flat and a ring of keys on his belt that did not make a sound when he moved. He held a ledger against his chest the way other people hold a baby. Mr Camel had seen him at the well many times over the years, counting the buckets as they came up, writing the numbers down in neat grey columns. He had always thought it a strange way to love water.

"Your Majesty," said Malquor, "honoured council. And a good morning to you, Mr Camel." His voice was polite and very precise, like a knife laid carefully on a plate. "The Singing Well is failing. I have measured it every day for twenty years and I can tell you that this was always going to happen. Every well grows old. Channels silt up. The desert drinks. We must not panic. We must manage."

"Manage how?" said Mr Camel.

"With a price." Malquor opened his ledger. "As it happens, a number of private cisterns in the city are still full. Their owners are willing, out of public spirit, to sell water to the people at a fair rate, until the well recovers. I have drawn up a schedule. One coin a bucket. Two for the palace, naturally, which can afford it."

There was a murmur round the table. Samira's crop tapped against the step.

"And who owns these cisterns?" asked Mr Camel.

Malquor smiled very slightly. "Only honest merchants of Camelamamm. They prefer not to be named. They are modest people."

Mr Camel thought of the grey-coated men he had seen in the last few years, buying up empty cisterns all over the city, cisterns that nobody had used since his grandmother's time. He had wondered at the time why anybody would pay for a hole full of dust. He did not wonder now.

"The well is not old," he said. "A well does not get old in a single night. Something has stopped it."

"With respect," said Malquor, "you sell tea."

"I do," Mr Camel agreed. "Before that I read maps for the caravans for twelve years. Before that I was taught by Ozra the Wellkeeper, who kept that well for almost sixty. She told me what to do if it ever went quiet. I am only sorry it took me so long to remember."

The room had gone still again. Malquor's fingers tightened on the ledger, just a little, and then relaxed.

"Ozra," he said, "was a very old camel with a great many stories."

"She was," said Mr Camel. "And she was never once wrong about water."

Samira stood up. She was not tall, but she had spent years throwing sacks onto caravan saddles and it showed in her shoulders, and when she stood up people tended to stop arguing.

"Steward, your schedule is noted," she said. "Nobody sells a single bucket until I say so. The palace cisterns open to everyone, free, from noon. Council is finished." And when the room had emptied, and only she and Mr Camel and one small fennec who had not been invited were left, she sat back down on the step and put her face in her hands.

"I do not trust him," she said through her fingers. "I have never trusted him. But I cannot throw out my Steward of Water on the morning the water stops because an old friend has a feeling."

"It is not a feeling," said Mr Camel. He lowered himself onto the step beside her, which took a while. "In 498 Ozra died at the edge of the well. Before she went she gave me two things. A map, and a key. She told me the water does not come from under the city at all. It comes a long way, through channels the Well Wardens dug before there was a Camelamamm, from a cave under the Glass Dunes. If the well ever went quiet, she said, I should go and look there, and not trust anybody who tells me to wait."

"You have waited fourteen years to tell me this?"

"There was nothing to tell. The well was singing."

Samira laughed, which was what he had wanted. Then she stopped laughing. "The Glass Dunes are four days by camel. The palace cisterns will last the city three."

"Then we will not go by camel," said Mr Camel.

Pip, who had been hiding behind a pillar and listening with both ears, stepped out. "I know someone with a skiff," she said. "He is rude and he smells of engine grease, but he owes you tea."

"Tobiah," said Mr Camel, and sighed, and smiled. "Yes. He does."
