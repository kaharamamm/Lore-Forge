---
title: The Humming Map
order: 3
date: 10.03.512
---

The Humming Map lived in a leather tube behind the tea tins, where Mr Camel had put it fourteen years ago and had taken it out, perhaps, twice.

He took it out that night by lamplight on the counter of the Patient Kettle, with the shutters closed and Pip sitting cross-legged on the counter beside it, and unrolled it slowly, because it was old goatskin and he did not want it to crack. It was an arm's length wide. Across it, in fine silver ink, Ozra had drawn the water: dozens of thin silver lines running south from a small green circle that was the Singing Well, branching and joining and branching again, out under the city walls and across the empty gold of the Sunsea, until they gathered together under a cluster of sharp little triangles that she had labelled, in her small stern handwriting, with one word: glass. Under the triangles was a hollow drawn in blue, and over the hollow a tiny key.

"It is just a map," said Pip, disappointed.

"Wait," said Mr Camel.

They waited. Pip lasted almost a minute before she opened her mouth, and Mr Camel put one long finger gently on her nose, and in the quiet after that they both heard it. A thin, high, silvery humming, like a fingertip run round the rim of a glass. It came from the lines. Mr Camel bent his head close, and the sound grew; he moved his ear along the channels, and in some places it was bright and steady, and in others it faded to almost nothing.

"It hums where there is water," he said. "Ozra drew it in ink from the well itself. Listen." He put his ear to the green circle that was the Singing Well. It was silent. He followed the main channel south, out of the city, a hand's width, two, three, all silent, all the way to the little blue hollow under the Glass Dunes. There, very faintly, the map was humming like a hive.

"The water is still there," said Pip. "It is just not coming."

"Something is holding it," said Mr Camel. "In the Hollow of Echoes."

He rolled the map up and slid it back into its tube. Then he reached under the counter, into a tin marked "mint, do not touch", and took out a key as long as his hand, made of green bronze worn smooth by years of thumbs. Its bow was shaped like a drop of water. Its bit had seven teeth.

"The Warden Key," he said. "Ozra wore it round her neck for sixty years. She said it opens the old sluice gate in the Hollow, and that it will not turn if you force it. You have to wait for it."

"Wait for a key?"

"It is a key of the Well Wardens. Everything they made was a little bit like that."

That was when the shutter broke.

It came in all at once, in a shower of blue-painted splinters, and three men came through it after it, wrapped in grey cloth so that only their eyes showed, with grey gloves on their hands. The first one went for the tube on the counter. Pip went for the first one. She was half his size and she hit him in the stomach with her head and her satchel at the same time, and he folded up with a noise like a dropped bag of flour.

"The Dry Hand!" said a voice in the dark. "The key, take the key!"

Mr Camel did not fight. He had never been a fighter; his body was the wrong shape for it, all knees and hump and patience. What he did instead was pick up the brass kettle, which had been sitting on its stove all evening at a gentle simmer, and pour it, very calmly, across the floor between himself and the other two men. They were wearing sandals. They went back out through the window a good deal faster than they had come in.

The one on the floor scrambled after them, coughing. At the window he turned and looked back, and his grey wrapping had slipped, and Mr Camel saw a face he knew from the bucket line at the well: one of Malquor's clerks, who stood beside the Steward every morning and held the ink.

Then he was gone, and the night was quiet again, and the kettle was empty.

"They knew about the key," said Pip. Her ears were flat against her head and her whole small body was shaking, but she had the tube clutched in both arms. "How did they know about the key?"

"Because Malquor has been listening to that well for twenty years," said Mr Camel slowly, "and I think he listened to Ozra too." He put the kettle down. His hands were not quite steady, so he gave them something to do; he filled the kettle from the jar, set it back on the stove, and lit the flame. "We are not waiting for morning. We need Tobiah."

"Now?"

"Now. And tell him he can complain all the way there."
