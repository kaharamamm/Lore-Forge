---
title: The Hollow of Echoes
order: 5
date: 13.03.512
---

Inside, every sound came back seven times.

Their footsteps came back seven times. Tobiah's muttering came back seven times, which made it seem as if a whole crew of small bearded men were complaining in the dark behind them. When Pip sneezed, the Hollow of Echoes sneezed back at her from every direction at once, and she clapped both paws over her muzzle and did not sneeze again.

They followed the tunnel down by lamplight. The walls were glass, blue-green and rippled like water, and the lamp made shapes in them that moved when they moved. The floor was wet. A little further in it became a channel, an old stone channel cut by hands, with a thin silver trickle running along the bottom of it towards the north, towards home. Mr Camel stopped and knelt and touched the water and put his finger to his tongue. It tasted of the Singing Well.

"That is all that is getting through," he said softly. "A trickle."

At the bottom of the tunnel the Hollow opened out into a cavern so big that the lamp could not find its roof. Water lay across the floor of it in a black, still lake, and the whole cavern hummed with it, so loud now that the map in Mr Camel's coat buzzed against his ribs like a bee. At the far end of the lake, where the channel began, stood a great gate of carved bronze, green with age, with water-birds on it like the ones on the canopy of the well. In the middle of the gate was a lock in the shape of a drop of water. And in front of the gate, built right across the mouth of the channel, there was a wall.

It was not old. It was made of grey stone blocks, neatly cut and neatly mortared, and it held the water back like a hand held over a mouth. On top of it, on a little platform with a lamp and a folding chair and a table with a ledger open on it, sat Malquor.

"Mr Camel," he said, and his voice came back seven times, precise and polite. "I wondered if you would come. I did not think you would come so fast. It is not like you."

"I had help," said Mr Camel.

Grey shapes moved on the far side of the lake: a dozen of them, two dozen, wrapped in grey cloth with grey gloves on their hands. The Dry Hand. They did not come closer. They did not need to.

"You built a dam," said Pip. Her voice shook, but it was loud, and the cavern made it louder. "You stopped the whole well. On purpose. The people are thirsty."

"People are always thirsty," said Malquor. "That is the beauty of water. For twenty years I watched this city take it for free, bucket after bucket after bucket, and I wrote down every single one, and do you know what I learned? That nobody values what they do not pay for." He tapped the ledger. "In a week the palace cisterns will be dry. In a week the Queen will sign my schedule herself, and thank me, and the Dry Hand will own every drop in the Sunsea. All I have to do is wait."

"Yes," said Mr Camel. "I understand waiting."

He walked out along the edge of the lake towards the bronze gate. Nobody stopped him; he was too slow to seem dangerous. When he reached the gate he took the Warden Key out of his coat, and it was very quiet in the Hollow suddenly, quiet enough to hear the water breathing.

"Ozra's key," said Malquor, and for the first time something in his voice was not polite. "Take it from him."

Two of the grey shapes started round the lake. Tobiah picked up a rock. Pip picked up a bigger one. Mr Camel put the key into the lock and turned it, and it did not move.

He did not force it. He had been told not to. He simply let go, and sat down on the wet stone in front of the gate with his long legs folded under him, the way he sat on the roof of the Patient Kettle every morning, and took a breath, and waited.

"It does not even work," said Malquor, and laughed, and the laugh came back seven times and sounded less sure of itself each time. "You came all this way to sit on the floor."

The grey shapes stopped. They looked at the camel sitting in front of the gate. They looked at each other. One of them, the clerk from the bucket line, lowered his hands.

"Get up," said Malquor. "Get up, you ridiculous animal."

Mr Camel did not get up. He thought about Ozra, who had kept this water for sixty years and never once asked for a coin for it. He thought about the bucket-seller who always measured, and the boy who said the well was tired, and Samira on the palace steps with her face in her hands. He thought about the kettle on the roof that sang every morning a moment after the well, as if it were answering. He listened to the water behind the grey wall, pressing and waiting, the way he was waiting. And slowly, slowly, without anyone touching it, the Warden Key began to turn.

It turned once. The lock clicked. It turned twice, and somewhere deep in the bronze gate a great bar slid back with a groan. It turned seven times, one for each tooth, and the gate swung open on the old channel, and the whole weight of the lake leaned against Malquor's wall from the side he had never thought to build for.

The wall cracked. It cracked again. Malquor stood up on his platform with his ledger clutched to his chest and his silver hair standing on end, and for a moment his face was not careful at all, it was only frightened.

"Jump," said Mr Camel kindly. "Towards us. The water will be going the other way."

Malquor jumped. Tobiah caught him by his grey silk collar and dropped him on the stone, and the wall went down behind him with a roar that came back seven times seven, and the water poured through the open gate into the channel of the Well Wardens and away to the north, towards home, so fast and so loud that the Hollow of Echoes seemed to be singing.

The grey shapes did not try to stop them. Most of them, Mr Camel saw, had sat down too.
