---
title: The Well Sings Again
order: 6
date: "14.03.512"
---

They flew home with the water.

It ran beside them the whole way, under the sand, and they could hear it. The map in Mr Camel's coat hummed so loudly that Tobiah made him sit on it. Malquor sat in the middle of the Grumbling Gull with his hands tied in front of him with Pip's spare sash and his ledger taken away, and said nothing at all, which Pip said was the best thing he had ever done. The clerk from the bucket line had asked to come too. He sat at the back, and when they stopped to rest he made the tea, and he made it badly, and Mr Camel drank it anyway, all three glasses.

They came over the last dune at dusk on the fourteenth and saw Camelamamm below them, blue and gold, with every terrace and roof crowded with people and every one of them looking the same way, towards the round stone canopy at the heart of the city. The palace cisterns had run dry that morning. Nobody was shouting.

Tobiah brought the Gull in through the south gate so fast that the runners threw sparks off the paving, and Mr Camel climbed out stiffly and walked to the well with his hands folded behind his back, and the crowd let him through.

He leaned over the green-glazed rim. Down in the dark, far below, the water was rising.

He felt it first in his hump, the way he always did. Then the bucket-seller by the steps felt it, and grabbed his neighbour's arm, and then the whole crowd felt it at once, and went silent, and listened. A low note came up out of the earth, deep and slow and very old, rising as the water rose, until it filled the canopy and the square and the Street of Kettles and the whole city on its hill. The Singing Well was singing. It was singing at midnight instead of dawn, which it had never done before and has never done since, and nobody in Camelamamm slept.

Queen Samira came down the palace hill with half the council running behind her and did not stop until she reached the well. She looked at Mr Camel, and at the water spilling silver over the green bricks and running between everybody's feet, and at Malquor standing between Tobiah and Pip with his hands tied in a blue courier sash. Then she laughed and laughed until she had to sit down on the steps.

That same night, by lantern-light, with water still running over her sandals, Samira had Malquor arrested and sent to the palace cells, and she declared the Dry Hand finished before the whole city. Its cisterns were opened. Its grey coats were given to the bucket-seller, who measured them and said they would make good sacks. The clerk from the bucket line went to work in the palace kitchens, where, it was hoped, somebody would teach him to make tea.

"You were right about all of it," Samira said, when the dancing had moved down the hill and they could hear themselves think. "I should have listened to you fourteen years ago."

"There was nothing to listen to," said Mr Camel. "The well was singing."

"It is singing now." She put her hand on the rim. "Ozra was the last of the Well Wardens. Somebody has to keep it."

Mr Camel looked at the water for a long time. He thought of the Patient Kettle, with its seven cushions and its one window and its sign over the door that said No hurry. He thought of the roof in the mornings. Then he thought that, in a way, he had been keeping the well already, every morning, for fourteen years, by listening to it.

"I will keep it," he said. "If I may keep the tea house too. And if I may have an apprentice."

Everybody looked at Pip. Pip looked at the bronze key hanging on its cord around Mr Camel's neck, and at the old indigo robe that Samira's steward was already holding out, and for once in her life she did not say anything fast.

"Me?" she said at last. "But I am impatient. I hate waiting. I am terrible at it."

"Yes," said Mr Camel. "That is why you will need a great deal of practice."

So in the year 512 of the Camelamamm Reckoning, the Well Wardens had two new members, which the old wardens would have called a great flood. Pip still carries the Queen's letters, but she carries them a little more slowly now, and in the mornings she climbs onto the roof of the Patient Kettle and sits beside Mr Camel and waits for the sun. She lasts almost a minute before she opens her mouth.

And every morning, as the first light touches the copper dome, a low note rises out of the middle of the city, so deep that you feel it before you hear it, and a moment later, on a little stove on a rooftop on the Street of Kettles, a brass kettle begins to sing.

Mr Camel says that is the kettle's idea. Pip says it is his. They have agreed to keep listening until they find out.
