---
title: Nobody Listens
order: 4
date: "03.03.527"
---

It took them less than an hour to get down the mountain, which was half the time it had ever taken Kerem before, and when they reached The Square of Bells they could hardly breathe.

The square was full. Every family in Kaharamamm had sent someone down to help with the Champion's Feast. The long tables ran from the river to the steps of The Hall of Heroes, and people were laying them with copper plates and jugs and bowls of red peppers and garlands of mountain flowers. Children were hanging paper lanterns from the strings of bells. The Bellguard in their bronze shirts were polishing the steps. The Tarnwater ran along the bottom of the square, so high now that it slapped against the top of its stone banks and threw cold spray over the nearest table, and the people laying that table moved the bread a little further along and went on laying it.

Vural Goldmane stood on the steps of the Hall with a long list in his paw, telling everybody where to put everything.

Kerem pushed through the crowd and up the steps. "Lord Herald," he said. "Lord Herald, the tarn is weeping."

Vural looked down. It took him a moment to place Kerem, and then his face lit up with the delight of a man who has remembered a good story. "The paddle-boy! The brave try! What can I do for you, young hero?"

"The Mirror Tarn. The ice wall is cracked all the way across. Water is coming out of it everywhere. A piece fell off while we were there, as big as a house. And there's a carving on a stone beside it, from the Seven, and it says, when the ice weeps, ring. It's going to break, Lord Herald. And when it breaks, all that water will come down the Tarnwater. Here."

He said it all in one breath, much too fast, and as he said it he heard how it sounded. A small boy who had fallen off a wall in front of the whole city yesterday, standing on the steps of the Hall the next morning with mud on his knees, telling everybody the mountain was falling down.

Several people laughed. Vural laughed too, but kindly, and patted Kerem on the head.

"My dear boy. The tarn weeps every Highheat. It has wept every Highheat since the Seven. That is what ice does in the sun." He waved his list at the square, at the tables, at the lanterns. "Tonight the whole city sits down here together to honour its champion. It is the best night of the year. Nobody wants to spend it standing on a cold mountain because a crack in some ice frightened a paddle-boy. Now, if you will excuse me..."

"Then ring the bell," said Kerem. "Just in case. Please. If the bell rings, everyone will go up to the Ridge, and..."

The laughter round them stopped. Vural stopped smiling, and for a moment he looked truly shocked.

"Ring the Hero's Bell? With what? It has no rope, child, and it has not had one for sixty years. It is not a dinner gong. It does not ring because somebody is frightened. It rings for heroes." He said the last word gently, the way you might explain something to a very young child, and that was the worst thing of all.

Baran Ironhorn had come up behind them. He was frowning, not at Kerem but at the river. "How wide was the crack?" he asked.

"All the way across."

"Captain," said Vural, without turning round. "The Bellguard stays in the square tonight. Every one of them. The whole city will be here, and I want every bronze shirt polished and every guard standing where I can see them. That is an order from the Hall."

Baran opened his mouth. He looked at Kerem, and at the river, and at the Lord Herald. Then he shut it again, and saluted, and walked away down the steps. But Kerem saw him stop at the bottom and look up, for a long time, at the mountain.

Kerem went home.

He told his grandmother everything, standing in the doorway of the stall with Ayla behind him, all of it: the creaking, the silver threads of water, the slab of ice, the carving with the seven lines, Vural's paw on his head. When he had finished, Nene Hatice did something he had never seen her do on a Feast day. She put down her paddle, walked to the front of the stall, and pulled down the shutters, right in the faces of the queue.

Then she sat down on her stool, with her stick between her knees, and was quiet for so long that Kerem began to think she had not heard him.

"I was fifteen," she said at last, "on the night of the Great Rockfall."

Kerem sat down on the floor. Ayla sat down beside him.

"It was a wet spring, like this one. I had gone up in the dark for snow, the way you do. My grandmother sent me. And on the way back down I heard the cliffs above the eastern terraces cracking, all along their length, the way this mountain is cracking now." She looked at her hands. "I did not stop to think. I was too frightened to think. I ran down to the Hall. In those days the tower door stood open, and the rope hung down from the bell all the way to the bottom of the stair, red and white, and anybody could ring it. So I pulled it. I hung off it with all my weight and pulled until my hands bled. And the bell rang, and the whole city woke up, and ran. And then the eastern terraces fell down where they had been sleeping."

"Nobody died," said Kerem. Everybody knew that part. It was the most famous part of the story.

"Nobody died," said his grandmother. "When I came out of the tower, the Herald of those days was standing on those same steps, telling everyone that the bell had rung by itself, for the heroes of Kaharamamm. And everyone was crying and hugging and singing. I went up to him and said, Herald, I rang it. He patted me on the head." She touched the place between her horns. "Just there. And then the singing got louder, and it seemed rude to keep saying it. So I stopped."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Two years after that, the Heralds took the rope down. A bell that rings by itself for heroes, they said, must never be rung by a common hand. They locked the tower door and kept the key. And in a little while, everybody forgot that the bell had ever had a rope at all."

She looked at Kerem, and her black eyes were bright and fierce.

"I should have shouted, Kerem. I was fifteen, and I let them pat my head, and I told myself it did not matter who had rung it as long as everyone was safe. But it did matter. Because they forgot what the bell was for." She leaned forward and put one hard old finger on his chest. "Do not let them pat yours."

"The door is locked," said Kerem. "The Lord Herald has the key on his belt."

"Then you will have to find another way up."

"There's another way up," said Ayla. They both looked at her. Her ears were flat, but her voice was steady. "The back of the tower, on the river side. The stones are rough and there are gaps in the mortar. I've climbed it as far as the ledge under the belfry. Never all the way."

"I fell off a wall yesterday," said Kerem.

"You fell off a wall with the whole city watching you," said Ayla. "Nobody will be watching this one. They'll all be eating."

Nene Hatice got up. She reached out and touched The Copper Paddle where it hung across Kerem's back: the old one, the one she had made with her own hands the year she opened the stall, with the copper bands round its handle worn smooth by fifty-seven years of hands.

"Good," she said. "You will need that. A bell with no rope still has a clapper, and the clapper has an iron ring at the bottom where the rope used to be tied. I know. I have held it." She picked up her stick. "Go on. I am going to talk to the Guild."
