---
title: The Weeping Ice
order: 3
date: "03.03.527"
---

Kerem did not want to go up the mountain the next morning.

He lay in bed in the dark and listened to the bells on the strings outside jingling in the wind, and thought about the straw, and the laughing, and the kind lamb-noise the crowd had made at the stone, and decided that he would stay under his blanket until he was at least thirty. Then he remembered that the Champion's Feast was tonight, that half the city would want snow-cream, and that snow did not fetch itself. He got up.

Ayla was waiting at the bottom of the long stair, sitting on the wall with her tail hanging down.

"You said you go up every morning," she said, as if that explained it. "I've never seen the tarn. My father says it's no place for a lamp-lighter's daughter." She hopped down. "I'm not a lamp-lighter's daughter today."

So they climbed together, up through the sleeping city, past the shuttered houses and the empty Ridge, out onto the cold grey shoulders of The Snowcrown. Ayla went up the steep places as if they were flat and waited for him at the top of each one, not impatiently. Kerem found that he did not mind. It was easier to climb behind somebody who did not think it was hard.

They heard the tarn before they saw it.

It was a sound Kerem had never heard before: a long, low creak that seemed to come from inside the mountain, like the door of a giant's house being opened very slowly. It stopped. Then, after a while, it came again, and with it a sharp crack like a whip, and the rattle of stones falling a long way off.

Ayla stopped on the path and her ears went flat. "What was that?"

Kerem did not answer. He was already running.

When he came over the last rise onto the snowfield he stopped so suddenly that Ayla nearly ran into him.

The Mirror Tarn was full to the brim. The water lay against the lip of the ice wall like milk in a bowl filled too high, trembling, and every so often a little wave slopped over the top and ran down the face of the wall in a glittering sheet. The grey line he had seen two mornings ago was not a line any more. It was a crack, black and jagged, running the whole length of the wall, and out of it, all along its length, water was spurting in thin silver threads, hissing, catching the first light. The whole wall was streaming. It looked, Kerem thought, exactly as if it were weeping.

Below the wall, where The Tarnwater began its long run down the gorge to the city, the river was brown and fast and much too high, and it was carrying stones.

"It's going to break," said Ayla, very quietly.

"It always looks like this in Highheat," said Kerem, but he could hear that it was not true even as he said it. He had come up here every morning since he was eight. It had never looked like this.

The wall creaked again, louder. A slab of ice the size of a house leaned slowly away from the top of it and fell into the lake, and a wave rolled across the tarn and broke over the lip of the wall in a white rush, and the whole wall shuddered, and held.

"Kerem," said Ayla. "Look."

She had climbed down onto the rocks at the end of the ice wall, where the old stone of the mountain met the old ice, and was crouching beside a great flat boulder half buried in lichen and snow. She was scraping at it with her claws. Kerem slithered down beside her.

Somebody had carved the boulder, a long time ago. The marks were worn almost smooth, and they had been full of lichen for so long that from more than a few steps away they looked like nothing at all. But close up, with the lichen scraped off, they were quite clear. At the top was a bell, the same shape as The Hero's Bell, with its mouth open. Below it were seven short upright lines, side by side, like seven people standing in a row. And below them were words, in the old square letters that nobody used any more except The Paddle Guild, who still wrote their recipes in them so that the other guilds could not read them.

Kerem could read them. His grandmother had taught him, with a slate, on winter evenings.

"What does it say?" said Ayla.

He traced the letters with a finger, slowly, to be sure.

"When the ice weeps," he read, "ring."

They looked at each other. Then they looked at the wall, weeping silver along the whole of its length, and at the dark water trembling behind it, and down past the snowfield and the bare slopes to the city far below, where the first smoke was rising from the chimneys and the banners for the Champion's Feast were being hung across The Square of Bells, right at the bottom of the gorge, right beside the river.

"The Seven knew," said Kerem. "They knew this could happen. That's what the bell is for."

"The bell rings for heroes," said Ayla, but slowly, as if she were hearing the words for the first time and did not much like the sound of them.

The ice wall groaned. It was a long, deep, tired sound, and Kerem felt it through the soles of his feet.

He left the snow pail where it was. They ran.
