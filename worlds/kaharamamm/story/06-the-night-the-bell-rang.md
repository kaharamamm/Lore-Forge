---
title: The Night the Bell Rang
order: 6
date: "04.03.527"
---

They rang until their arms shook, and then they rang some more.

Kerem lost count of the strokes. After a while he stopped hearing them and only felt them: the long backward drag, the letting go, the great bronze shudder that went through the floor and the beam and his bones. Between strokes he leaned out and watched the square empty. The lanterns climbed the long stair in a slow bright stream. The tables stood deserted under the strings of bells, still covered in food. The river had come over its banks now and was spreading across the bottom of the square in a sheet of black water, nosing among the table legs, picking up a chair and turning it round, thoughtfully, as if deciding where to put it.

Then there were footsteps on the stair below them, inside the tower, heavy and fast.

The trapdoor in the belfry floor banged open and Baran Ironhorn's horns came through it, and then the rest of Baran Ironhorn, and then, puffing and gasping and with his mane in a terrible state, Vural Goldmane, holding a large iron key.

For a moment the Lord Herald only stood there, staring at the bell, and at the two children, and at the paddle through the clapper's ring. Kerem saw his eyes go to the place where the green crust had been rubbed away, and to the words underneath it. He read them. His lips moved.

"Who rings me," said Vural. And then, in a different voice, very quietly: "Oh."

"That will do, bell-ringers," said Baran. "Everyone is on the stair. It is time we were on it too."

They went down the inside of the tower, which was much quicker than coming up the outside. At the bottom the water was already pouring in under the door, and by the time they were out in the square it was over Kerem's knees, cold as snow, and pulling. Baran picked him up and set him on his shoulders without asking. Ayla, who had no intention of being carried by anybody, ran along the tops of the floating tables instead. Vural waded behind them in his velvet robe, which drank up the water and grew heavier with every step, until halfway across the square he slipped and went under.

Kerem held out The Copper Paddle. Vural grabbed the end of it. Between them, Kerem and Baran hauled him up, coughing, and he held on to the paddle all the way to the stair, and did not let go of it until they reached the top.

They were the last ones up. When they came out onto The Ridge of the Seven, the whole of Kaharamamm was there: eighteen thousand people standing on the high terraces in the dark, wrapped in blankets and tablecloths, holding lanterns and children and each other, and all of them looking up at the mountain.

The Mirror Tarn broke a little after midnight.

They did not see it. They heard it: a long, tearing crack, and then a roar that went on and on, growing, coming closer, until it filled the whole gorge. Then The Tarnwater came down out of the dark, white and brown and higher than a house, carrying ice and trees and boulders as if they were twigs. It went through the bottom of the city like a fist through paper. It went through The Square of Bells. It picked up the long tables of the Champion's Feast and the strings of lanterns and the copper plates and carried them away. It went in through the great doors of The Hall of Heroes, and filled the Hall to the knees of The Seven Founders, and came out of the windows on the other side. It climbed the market street, step by step, swallowing stalls, until it stopped, at last, one step below The Long Paddle, and lay there sulking in the lantern light.

And the bell tower stood. All night the water pulled at it, and all night it stood, and every so often, when a big wave struck it, the bell inside gave a long low hum, as if it were talking to itself.

Nobody on the Ridge slept. Baran Ironhorn went from terrace to terrace with a list, street by street, house by house, counting. It took him until the sky went grey. When he had finished he climbed onto a wall where everyone could see him and held up the list.

"Everyone," he said, and his great voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Every single one."

Nobody cheered. They were too tired, and too cold, and too amazed. Instead, all along the Ridge, people turned to each other and held on.

In the morning the water went down.

The bottom of Kaharamamm was a ruin of mud and stones. The square lay under a grey sludge full of broken tables and lanterns. Inside the Hall of Heroes the statues of the Seven stood in mud to their knees, and one of them had toppled onto its face. Kahara the Bellmaker stood in the middle of them, still holding her stone bell, with a string of lanterns caught round her horns.

Vural Goldmane went into the Hall alone. When he came out he was carrying something over his shoulder: a long thick coil of rope, red and white, grey with nearly sixty years of dust.

"The Bell Rope," he said, to the people who had gathered in the mud to watch him. "My grandfather took it down. It has been in the Heralds' vault ever since. I have walked past it every day of my life." He looked at the rope for a long moment. "I will put it back."

And he did. He climbed the inside stair of the tower himself, with the rope on his shoulder and Baran to help him, and tied it to the iron ring of the clapper with his own paws, and let it fall all the way to the bottom of the tower, where anyone could reach it.

Then Baran Ironhorn took off his white champion's sash, and walked across the mud, and held it out to Kerem.

Kerem looked at it. Then he looked round at the crowd, at all the muddy, tired, living faces of Kaharamamm, and found his grandmother among them, small and white-headed, leaning on her stick at the back.

"It isn't mine," he said. And then, because nobody was patting his head and nobody was going to, he said it louder, so that everyone could hear. "In the Great Rockfall, nobody died because somebody rang the bell. It didn't ring by itself. Nene Hatice rang it. She was fifteen. Nobody asked her."

There was a silence. Everybody turned to look at the old snow-cream maker at the back of the crowd. Nene Hatice looked back at them all, and sniffed.

"Hang it on the bell," she said. "It's the bell's."

So the sash was hung on the bell rope, at the bottom of the tower, where it still hangs. There were no Hero Trials in Kaharamamm the next year, or the year after, or ever again. Instead, every morning before dawn, two of The Bellguard climb to the bowl of rock where the Mirror Tarn used to be, and look at the ice, and come down again. And every morning, as the sun comes over The Snowcrown, somebody rings The Hero's Bell, once, so that the city remembers what it sounds like and what it is for.

The Long Paddle opened again three days after the flood, with mud still drying on its step. The first customer in the queue was Vural Goldmane, who asked for one snow-cream, please, and said thank you twice. Kerem stretched the scoop out across the street on the end of The Copper Paddle, all the way to the other side, further than he had ever stretched one before, and whipped it away just as Vural reached for it, and gave it back, and took it again, three times, with his face perfectly straight. The whole queue laughed. Vural laughed loudest of all.

Every morning in Kaharamamm begins with a bell now, and every morning Kerem is the one who rings it, on his way up the mountain to fetch the snow.
