---
title: The Hero Trials
order: 2
date: "02.03.527"
---

The Hero Trials began at noon, when the sun stood straight over The Square of Bells and there was no shade anywhere to hide in.

The whole of Kaharamamm came down to watch. They packed the square and the steps of The Hall of Heroes and the roofs all round, and those who could not find room on the roofs sat along the walls of the terraces above with their legs dangling, eating roasted chestnuts and peppers on sticks and shouting advice. The Tarnwater ran loud and brown along the bottom of the square, fat with snowmelt. Nobody listened to it. It was always loud in Highheat.

There were twenty-three entrants, and Kerem was the smallest by a head and a half. He stood at the end of the line in his best red waistcoat with a number pinned to it and tried not to look at Baran Ironhorn, who stood at the other end like a wall that had decided to come to the Trials.

Vural Goldmane climbed onto a barrel with his brass speaking-trumpet and told the story of the bell, which everybody knew, and then told it again, louder, with gestures, which everybody also knew. "Three feats!" he cried. "The Stone, for strength! The Wall, for courage! The Stair, for endurance! And for the champion, the walk beneath the Hero's Bell. People of Kaharamamm, will this be the year?"

"THE YEAR!" roared the city, because it always did.

The first feat was the great round stone that stood by the river, smooth as an egg and grey as a thundercloud, which The Seven Founders were said to have rolled down from the mountain with their bare hands. You had to lift it to your chest and hold it there while the Lord Herald counted to ten. Most of the entrants got it to their knees. Three got it to their waists. Baran Ironhorn lifted it to his chest, held it for ten, and then, when the crowd kept cheering, held it for ten more, looking faintly embarrassed.

Kerem bent his knees, wrapped his arms round the stone, and pulled until he saw little white stars. The stone thought about it. Then it rocked, very slightly, towards him, and settled back exactly where it had been.

There was a sound from the crowd that was not quite laughter. It was worse. It was the warm, kind sound people make at a lamb trying to climb stairs.

The second feat was the wall. The carpenters had built it against the side of the square: a tall face of planks with wooden handholds nailed on, twice the height of a house, with a heap of straw at the bottom and a little bronze bell at the top that you rang to show you had arrived. Kerem had been looking forward to the wall. He climbed the snowfields every morning. He was light, and he did not mind heights, and he had practised on the pepper terraces until his fingers were raw.

He started well. He went up the first half of the wall so fast that the crowd stopped making the lamb noise and began, a little, to cheer. Then, halfway up, he made a mistake. He looked down.

Below him was the whole of Kaharamamm. Thousands of faces, all turned up towards him, all watching. He saw Vural with his trumpet. He saw the people on the roofs with their chestnuts. He saw, at the edge of the crowd, a white headscarf, and knew that his grandmother had shut the stall and come down after all. And suddenly the handholds under his fingers felt very small and very far apart, and he could not remember how he had got this high, or how anybody ever got any higher.

He reached for the next hold, and missed it, and fell into the straw.

This time the crowd did laugh, a great surprised shout of it, not unkind but very loud. Vural's trumpet said, "A brave try from the paddle-boy! Give him a hand!" and they gave him a hand, which was worse than the laughing. Kerem lay in the straw looking up at the sky and wished, very hard, that he were a stone.

"You looked down," said a voice.

He turned his head. Sitting on the edge of the straw with her long tail wrapped round her feet was a snow-leopard girl with a red scarf wound twice round her neck and lamp-soot on her fingers. He knew her a little. Her name was Ayla, and her father lit the lamps in the Hall of Heroes, and she was always somewhere above you: on a roof, on a wall, on the head of one of the stone Founders, where she was not allowed to be.

"Everybody was watching," said Kerem.

"That's why you don't look at them. You look at the stone." She held out a hand and pulled him up. "Walls are better when nobody's watching. That's why I don't enter."

"You'd win."

Ayla shrugged, as if winning were a thing that happened to other people. "You've still got the Stair," she said.

The Stair was a race: up the long stair from the square to The Ridge of the Seven, a thousand steps and more, round the old marker stone at the top, and down again. Baran Ironhorn won it so easily that he had time to stop on the way and carry an old goat-folk woman's shopping into her house. The others came in one by one, red-faced and blowing. Kerem came in last, a long time after everybody, when the shadows had crossed the square and half the crowd had gone to look for dinner. But he came in. Ayla was sitting on the bottom step, waiting for him. So, a little way off, was a white headscarf.

Then it was time for the walk.

The square went quiet. Baran Ironhorn took off his helmet, tucked it under his arm, and walked slowly across the cobbles to the arch at the foot of the bell tower. Above him, high in the open belfry, The Hero's Bell hung green and still against the evening sky. Everybody in Kaharamamm held their breath. Kerem held his too. He could not help it.

Baran walked under the arch. He stopped. He stood there, under the bell, for a long time.

The Tarnwater rushed past at the end of the square. A pigeon clapped its wings in the belfry. Somewhere up on the terraces, a goat complained.

The bell said nothing.

"Patience!" cried Vural Goldmane, before the silence could get too long. "The bell is patient, and so are we! People of Kaharamamm, I give you your champion, six times over! And next year, next year, who knows?"

The city cheered, because it always did. Baran smiled and waved, because he always did. But Kerem was close enough to see his face when he came out from under the arch, and it was not the face of a champion. It was the face of someone who had been told, very politely, for the sixth time, that he was not quite what was wanted.

Later, when the square was emptying and the lanterns were being lit, the big ram came and sat down heavily on the bottom step beside Kerem and Ayla, and stretched out his legs, and sighed.

"Six times," he said, to nobody in particular. "Six times I've walked under it."

"Maybe it's broken," said Kerem.

"Maybe I am." Baran turned his helmet round and round in his hands. "Do you know what I think, paddle-boy? I think it is not listening for what we think it is listening for. I just don't know what that is." He stood up, and nodded to them both, and went off across the square to be congratulated.

When Kerem got home his grandmother did not say I told you so. She put a bowl of snow-cream in front of him, the stretchy kind, with a little crushed pistachio on top, and sat down across the table with her stick between her knees.

"I was last," said Kerem, into the bowl.

"You finished," said Nene Hatice. "Eat."
