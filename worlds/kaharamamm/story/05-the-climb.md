---
title: The Climb
order: 5
date: "03.03.527"
---

By the time the sun went down behind The Snowcrown, the whole of Kaharamamm was sitting at the long tables in The Square of Bells.

Lanterns glowed on every string. The copper bells over the square tinkled in the evening wind. There were roast lamb and pepper bread and great dishes of rice with apricots, and fiddlers on the steps of The Hall of Heroes, and Vural Goldmane at the head of the top table with his mane brushed out to twice its usual size, rising every few minutes to make another speech. Baran Ironhorn sat beside him in his white sash, not eating. The Bellguard stood in a polished bronze ring round the edges of the square, just as they had been ordered to. Every so often one of them glanced up at the dark mountain, and then back at the Lord Herald, and stayed where they were.

Nobody was looking at the back of the bell tower.

It stood on the river side of the Hall, where a narrow ledge of stone ran between the wall and The Tarnwater. Kerem and Ayla stood on the ledge in the dark with the river roaring past their feet. At noon it had been a man's height below the ledge. Now it was slapping over the edge of it, ice-cold, soaking their boots. The noise was so loud they had to put their heads together to talk.

"Look at the stone," said Ayla into his ear. "Not down. Not at the people. The stone. Put your hands where I put mine."

Then she went up the wall like smoke.

Kerem tightened the strap that held The Copper Paddle across his back, put his hands where hers had been, and followed.

The stones of the tower were rough and old and wet with spray, and the gaps between them were just wide enough for fingers and the tips of hooves. For the first part he did not think at all. He watched Ayla's tail above him, swinging slowly from side to side as she climbed, and put his hands where she had put hers, and his feet where his hands had been, and went up. The noise of the river fell away beneath him. The noise of the feast came round the corner of the tower: music, laughter, the clink of plates, Vural's voice rising and falling.

Halfway up, a stone moved under his foot.

It slid out of the wall with a little gritty sigh and dropped away into the dark, and his hoof went with it, and for a moment he hung by his fingers with nothing under him at all. His heart stopped. He thought of the straw, and the shout of laughter, and the thousands of faces turned up towards him.

He looked down.

There was nobody there. There was only the black river, running fast and white-edged far below, and the dark wall of the Hall, and the glow of the lanterns round the corner, where everybody was eating and nobody was watching him at all. For the first time since he had decided to enter the Trials, nobody was watching Kerem.

He found that he did not need them to.

He looked at the stone in front of his face. It had a crack in it, just wide enough for two fingers. He put two fingers in it, found a new place for his foot, and went on climbing.

Ayla was waiting for him on the ledge below the belfry, crouched with her tail wrapped round her. She did not say anything. She just held out her hand and pulled him up the last bit, and they climbed together over the stone sill into the belfry of the tower.

It was much bigger inside than it looked from the square. The floor was thick with old straw and pigeon feathers. The wind came through the four open arches and smelled of snow. And in the middle, hanging from a great black beam, was The Hero's Bell.

Kerem had seen it every day of his life, from every street in the city. He had never been close to it. It was taller than Baran Ironhorn and wider than a doorway, green with age, and cold, and it hung so still that it seemed less like a thing that could ring than like a piece of the mountain someone had hung up to keep. Round its rim, in the old square letters, ran a line of writing. The words on the side that faced the square, the side the whole city could see, said I RING FOR THE HERO, and Kerem knew them, because they were stamped on all the little copper bells the Heralds sold.

But the writing did not stop there. It went on round the rim, onto the side that faced the back wall, where nobody in the square could see it and where sixty years of weather had covered it with a thick green crust. Kerem rubbed at the crust with his sleeve. It came away in flakes.

WHO RINGS ME, said the bell.

"I ring for the hero who rings me," Kerem read aloud. His voice sounded very small.

Ayla looked at the words for a long moment. Then she looked at him. "Well," she said. "Then ring it."

The clapper hung down inside the bell's mouth: a great iron tongue as heavy as a goat, and at the bottom of it, just as Nene Hatice had said, an iron ring, rusted, where the rope had once been tied. Kerem unstrapped The Copper Paddle and pushed its long handle through the ring until it stuck out on both sides. Ayla took one end. He took the other.

"Back," he said. They walked backwards, dragging the clapper with them, until it would come no further. "And... now!"

They let go.

The clapper swung across the mouth of the bell and struck the far side.

The sound was not a sound. It was a weather. It came out of the bell and filled the belfry and knocked the pigeons off the beam and went out through the four arches in every direction at once, down into the city and up into the mountain and out over the dark country beyond, so huge and so deep that Kerem felt it in his teeth and his horns and the soles of his feet. Every little copper bell on every string in Kaharamamm shivered and rang with it, a thousand small voices under one enormous one.

Below them, the feast stopped.

They pulled the clapper back and let it go again, and again, and again. Between the strokes Kerem leaned out over the sill and looked down into The Square of Bells. Every face was turned up. Vural Goldmane was standing on the top table with his speaking-trumpet in his paw and his mouth wide open, shouting something about heroes, about a miracle, about the bell ringing at last, and looking wildly round the square to see who had walked beneath it.

But Baran Ironhorn was not looking round the square. He was looking straight up at the belfry, where two small figures stood against the lantern light with a snow-cream paddle between them. And then, in the silence between two strokes of the bell, the mountain groaned, long and deep, loud enough for everybody to hear.

Baran stood up on his chair. Then he stood up on the table. Then he filled his great chest and used the voice that could carry across the whole Square of Bells.

"UP!" he roared. "Everyone UP! Up to the Ridge! Bellguard, to the stair! The tarn is breaking! UP!"

For a moment nobody moved. Then everybody moved at once. Chairs went over. Children were snatched up. The Bellguard broke their polished ring and ran to the foot of the long stair and began to send people up it, quickly, steadily, family by family. And down the market street, through the crowd, came Nene Hatice with her stick held high over her head, and behind her the whole of The Paddle Guild, carrying their long paddles in pairs with aprons knotted between them, so that every two paddles made a stretcher. They went from table to table and lifted onto them the people who could not climb a thousand steps quickly: the very old, the very young, a goat-folk grandmother with a broken leg, a pair of twins too small to walk. Then they turned and carried them up the stair.

Kerem and Ayla went on ringing. Below them, in the dark, a river of lanterns was flowing uphill.
