---
title: Snow Before Sunrise
order: 1
date: "01.03.527"
---

Every morning in Kaharamamm began with snow, and every morning Kerem was the one who went up the mountain to fetch it.

He left the house while the stars were still out, with a copper pail in one hand and The Copper Paddle strapped across his back, and he climbed. Past the shuttered stalls of The Copper Market, past the pepper terraces where strings of red peppers hung drying from every roof like rows of little lanterns, up the long stair to The Ridge of the Seven, and on, out of the city altogether, onto the bare grey shoulders of The Snowcrown. By the time the sky turned pale he was standing on the snowfield above The Mirror Tarn, breathing clouds, with the whole of Kaharamamm spread out below him like a red blanket somebody had thrown down the mountainside and never bothered to straighten.

Nene Hatice said that snow-cream was only as good as its snow, and that the only good snow was snow nobody had stepped on yet. So Kerem walked to the far edge of the field, where the snow lay untouched and faintly blue, and knelt, and filled the pail with the flat of the paddle the way she had taught him: from the top, never from underneath, and never, ever with his hands.

Below him the Mirror Tarn lay perfectly still. It was a round lake the colour of an old mirror, cupped in a bowl of rock high above the city and held there by a great curved wall of ice and stones that the mountain had built for itself long before anybody lived on its slopes. In winter the lake froze white. Now, on the first morning of Highheat, it was open and dark, and higher than Kerem had ever seen it. The water came almost to the lip of the ice wall. A hand's breadth below the top there was a line where the ice had gone grey and glassy, as if it were tired.

Kerem looked at the line for a long moment. Then the sun came over the mountain, and the whole lake flashed silver, and he remembered what day it was and forgot about the ice altogether.

It was the first day of the Feast of Bells. Tomorrow were the Hero Trials. And this year, for the first time in his life, Kerem was old enough to enter.

He came down the mountain so fast that he had to hold the pail against his chest to keep the snow in it.

By then the Copper Market was awake and shouting. Kaharamamm was a steep city and its market was steep too: one long crooked street of stalls climbing the middle terraces in steps, so that the man who sold copper pots stood with his boots at the height of the head of the woman who sold peppers below him, and they could argue about the price of things without either of them having to look up. For the Feast, everything had been hung with bells. Copper bells on strings across the street, bells on the doorposts, bells on the awnings, bells on the goats. The whole market tinkled whenever the wind moved.

The Long Paddle was the third stall from the top. It was not the biggest snow-cream stall in the market, or the newest, or the one with the most bells. It was simply the one with the longest queue.

"You are late," said Nene Hatice, without turning round.

"The sun is only just up."

"The sun is not making snow-cream." His grandmother took the pail, looked into it, sniffed it, and grunted, which was as close as she came to praise. She tipped the snow into the cold copper tub sunk in the counter, where the goat's milk and the mountain honey were already waiting, added a handful of orchid flour and a pinch of tear-resin from the tin in her apron pocket, and nodded at the paddle on his back. "Beat."

Kerem beat.

Snow-cream was not like the soft sweet ices the caravan drivers talked about in the lowland cities. It had to be worked, slowly, for the best part of an hour, until the orchid flour and the resin turned it thick and glossy and so stubborn that a good scoop could be lifted on the end of a paddle and pulled out into a rope as long as your arm without breaking. The best makers of The Paddle Guild could do tricks with it. They would hold out a cone to a customer and whip the cream away on the paddle just as the customer reached for it, then give it back, then take it again, until the customer laughed and the whole queue laughed too. Nene Hatice could stretch a single scoop from one side of the street to the other. Kerem could stretch one about as far as his elbow, on a good day, when nobody was watching.

His grandmother was the eldest of the Paddle Guild and also the smallest. She was seventy-six years old, white as the snow she sold, with long ringed horns, one of them crooked at the tip, and black eyes that missed nothing. She had a walking stick of black juniper that she mostly used for pointing at things she disapproved of, and this morning it was pointing down the hill.

"Look at it," she said. "All that fuss."

From the top of the market you could see straight down to the bottom of the city, where The Tarnwater ran out of its gorge and past The Square of Bells. The square was being dressed for the Feast. Red and white banners hung from every window. Carpenters were building a climbing wall of planks against one side, and long tables were being stacked ready for the Champion's Feast on the last night. On the far side stood The Hall of Heroes, with the stone statues of The Seven Founders along its front, and Kahara the Bellmaker in the middle of them, holding a stone bell against her chest. Above the Hall rose the bell tower, and at the top of the tower, in the open belfry, hung The Hero's Bell: green-bronze, enormous, and as silent as it had been for sixty-one years.

Everyone in Kaharamamm knew the story of the bell. Kahara had cast it when the city was founded, and it rang for heroes. Not when you pulled a rope, because it had no rope. It rang by itself, when a true hero walked beneath it. It had rung like that on the night of the Great Rockfall, sixty-one years ago, for all the heroes of the city at once, and it had not rung since. Every year, at the Feast of Bells, the winner of the Hero Trials walked through the arch under the tower, and every year the whole city held its breath, and every year the bell said nothing at all.

"It might ring this year," said Kerem.

"Might it."

"It might ring for me."

His grandmother stopped stretching a scoop for a customer and looked at him, and the scoop hung in the air between them like a white rope.

"Heroes are only people who happened to be there, and did not run," she said. "They do not practise."

"Baran Ironhorn practises."

"And the bell has said nothing to Baran Ironhorn five years running." She flicked the scoop into a cone and handed it over the counter without looking. "Beat."

Just then the bells on the strings over the street began to jingle more than the wind could account for, and the queue shuffled aside, and up the market came the Lord Herald of the Hall himself.

Vural Goldmane was a lion-folk with a mane like a haystack at sunset. He wore crimson velvet trimmed with white fur, and a chain of tiny copper bells round his neck that rang when he walked, so that you always heard him a little before you saw him. Behind him came two of The Heralds of the Hall with trays of little copper bells for sale, each one stamped with the face of Kahara, and behind them, stooping under the strings of bells as though they were low doorways, came the biggest person in Kaharamamm.

Baran Ironhorn was ram-folk, dark brown, with horns that curled round twice and still had room to spare. He was the Captain of The Bellguard and the champion of the Hero Trials, and he wore the white champion's sash he had won five years in a row. He bought a snow-cream from The Long Paddle every morning of the Feast, and he always said thank you twice.

"Hatice!" cried Vural, spreading his arms. "Eldest of the Guild! Flower of the market! Two of your finest, if you please, and one for the Captain. It is a great Feast this year. I feel it in my mane."

Nene Hatice stretched his scoop out over the street, and whipped it away just as his paw reached it, and gave it back, and took it again, three times, with her face perfectly straight, until the whole queue was laughing. Vural laughed loudest of all. It was one of the things that made him hard to dislike.

"I am entering the Trials," Kerem told Baran, while the Lord Herald was busy being laughed at.

Baran looked down at him from a great height. He did not laugh. "Are you, now," he said. "Then I will see you on the wall, paddle-boy."

"A paddle-boy in the Hero Trials!" said Vural, who heard everything eventually. "Splendid. Splendid! The bell loves a story." And he jingled away down the hill.

That night Kerem lay awake in the little room above the stall, too excited to sleep. His mother and father were away across The Sunsea with the snow-cream caravans, packing ice-jars in straw for the tea houses of Camelamamm, nine days to the south, and he had already decided that he would write to them tomorrow and tell them that the bell had rung. Just before he fell asleep he thought, for a moment, about the grey line on the ice wall above the city.

Then he thought about the bell instead, and dreamed about it all night.
